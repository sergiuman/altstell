# Part IV: The Voices of the Valley (Rules of Listening and Speech)

---

## Chapter 8: The Owl's Listening

A grey mist was rolling in from the lake, wrapping the roots of the willow in a soft, quiet blanket. Kiri and Pip sat near the stone wall, watching Mr. Bramble argue with a small, brown woodmouse named Barnaby.

They were looking at the drainage channel Mr. Bramble had dug near the garden.

"It must be three inches deep!" Mr. Bramble barked, his tail slapping the mud. "I have calculated the slope. I know how the water flows. A mouse does not understand engineering!"

"But Mr. Bramble," Barnaby squeaked, his voice so quiet it was almost lost in the wind. "The soil under the moss is... it is different. It is not clay. If you dig it too deep, the water will..."

"Nonsense!" the beaver interrupted, waving a paw. "Water always behaves the same way. The channel stays where it is. I have no time to listen to whispers when there is work to be done."

Barnaby sighed, his whiskers drooping, and began to walk away, his head low.

From a high branch of the ancient willow, a great, grey owl named Rowan opened his yellow eyes. He looked down at Mr. Bramble, then at the retreating mouse.

"Bramble," Rowan hooted, his voice rich and slow. "When you build a dam, do you ask the stones where they want to lie?"

"Of course not," Mr. Bramble grunted. "Stones have no voices."

"But Barnaby has a voice," the owl said, drifting down to a lower branch. "And he has lived in the roots of this valley since the great oak was a sapling. When you speak, Bramble, you only repeat what you already know. But when you listen, you might learn something you do not know. Assume that the creature you are listening to has a secret to share, even if they are small."

Mr. Bramble stopped. He looked at Rowan, then at the little mouse who was almost invisible in the grass. He clicked his teeth, silent for a moment.

"Fine," the beaver muttered. "Mouse, come back. What about the soil?"

Barnaby turned around, his tail twitching. "The soil under the moss is sandy clay, Mr. Bramble. If you dig three inches deep, you will hit the sand layer. The water will not flow to the lake; it will sink into the sand and wash away the garden roots from underneath. We must make the channel wide and shallow, lining it with flat pebbles, not deep."

Mr. Bramble blinked. He knelt down, scraped away the moss with his claw, and peered into the dirt. Just two inches down, the dark earth turned to pale, loose sand. If he had dug another inch, the entire bank would have collapsed during the next rain.

The beaver stood up, his face softening. He looked at Barnaby with a new expression — not annoyance, but respect.

"You are right, mouse," Mr. Bramble said. "I did not know the sand was so shallow here. I would have ruined the walkway. I am sorry I did not listen."

"It's okay," Barnaby squeaked, his whiskers perking up. "We all want the walkway to stand."

Rowan closed his eyes, returning to his quiet perch. "The valley is full of secrets, Bramble. The owl sees the night, the mouse sees the roots, the beaver sees the logs. If you do not listen to the others, you will only see your own shadow."

Pip looked at Kiri, his tail twitching. "I guess... I should listen to the bluejays more. Even if they are noisy."

"We should all listen," Kiri smiled, drawing a small mouse holding a flat pebble. "Because everyone we meet knows something we don't."

## Chapter 9: The Messenger Bird

By the next afternoon, a cold wind was blowing from the north, and the sky was a sheet of heavy, grey slate. A young bluejay came screeching through the meadow, landing on Sola’s branches with a wild flutter.

"A monster! A monster is coming!" the jaybird screamed, his eyes wide. "It is giant! It has ten legs, and it is tearing down the trees at the edge of the valley! It is the end of the forest! We must all run! Run to the hills!"

A sudden panic swept through the clearing. The rabbits scrambled for their holes, the hedgehogs rolled into tight balls, and the field mice began to run in circles, dropping their winter seeds.

Pip bounced onto a branch, his tail shaking. "A monster? With ten legs? Kiri, we must pack our acorns! We must run!"

Kiri stood up, her face tense, but she looked at Sola. The ancient willow was not shaking; her leaves were calm.

"Where did you see this monster, jay?" Kiri asked.

"Near the eastern birch grove!" the jaybird cried, his wings trembling. "It was huge! It was crashing through the brush, making a terrible roaring sound! Trees were falling!"

"Kiri," Sola’s voice hummed, cool and steady, like a deep well. "When the wind blows, does it make the forest a monster? When a tree falls, is it always a giant?"

"No," Kiri said, her breathing slowing down. She looked at the bluejay. "Tell us exactly, jay. What did you see? Not what you felt, but what did you *see*?"

"It... it was dark," the jaybird stammered, looking confused. "And it was moving. It had many branches... I mean legs. And it was carrying wood."

"Was it Mr. Bramble?" Kiri asked.

The jaybird blinked. "Mr. Bramble? But he doesn't have ten legs!"

"No," Kiri said. "But he carries a large frame of birch poles on his back to make the walkway arches. When he walks through the brush with five poles on each side, he looks like he has ten legs."

Right on cue, a heavy rustling came from the eastern path. Mr. Bramble emerged from the trees, carrying a large bundle of birch branches strapped to his back. The poles stuck out on both sides, swaying as he walked, making him look like a strange, multi-legged forest creature. He set the bundle down with a grunt.

"Tiresome walk," the beaver muttered. "The branches kept catching on the weeds."

The forest creatures stopped running. The rabbits popped their heads out of their holes, and the mice looked at Mr. Bramble, then at the bluejay, who had turned a very bright shade of blue.

"Oh," the jaybird whispered. "It was just Bramble."

"Your words were not precise, jay," Sola’s leaves rustled, a gentle warning in their sound. "When you speak without precision, you turn a shadow into a giant, a twig into a spear, and a beaver into a monster. You spread fear because you did not take the time to name the thing exactly as it was. Speak with precision, little bird. For imprecise words make the world a chaotic and terrifying place."

Pip let out a sigh, his tail fluffing back to its normal shape. "Well," he said, "I'm glad we don't have to pack the acorns."

"So am I," Kiri smiled. "And next time we hear about a monster, we will ask for its exact name."
